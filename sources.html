What magnification buys

A detector has a horizon, and it is set by how loud a signal is when it arrives. A lens moves that horizon. A source that would have been too faint to register can be magnified into a confident detection, which means the lensed part of the sky is systematically deeper than the rest of it — and the further out a population lies, the larger the fraction of it that is seen only because something magnified it.{% include cite.html key="wierda-rates,smith-rates" %}

That is what makes lensing a route to the early Universe rather than only a curiosity about the lens. Binaries beyond a redshift of about three are near the edge of what current detectors reach unaided; magnified, they come into range. Further back still are the remnants of the first stars, whose masses and merger rate are among the sharper predictions distinguishing models of how the first generation of stars formed — and which are otherwise out of reach until the next generation of instruments.

Where the standard picture stops applying

The usual treatment of lensing assumes a great deal: that the field is weak, that deflections are small, that the lens is thin, and that the background it sits on is otherwise flat. Those assumptions hold for a wave passing a galaxy at a comfortable distance. They do not hold for a source in the vicinity of a supermassive black hole, where the wave is generated and lensed within the same strongly curved region.

Two things change there. The deflection is no longer a small correction to a straight path, so images can appear well inside the regime where the standard formalism was derived. And at first order beyond geometric optics the wave's polarization couples to the background curvature, so the two polarizations no longer follow quite the same trajectory — the arrival time acquires a dependence on both frequency and polarization. Sources near a supermassive black hole are therefore not simply brighter versions of ordinary ones; they are a different calculation.

This is the strong-field end of the project, and it is deliberately narrow: what the grant commits to is finding these events and interpreting them with a formalism that does not quietly assume the weak-field limit, rather than a programme of strong-field lensing in its own right.

Why a magnified source is hard to claim

Magnification on its own is invisible. A signal that has been amplified by a factor of ten looks exactly like an identical source ten times closer, or like a heavier source further away — the magnification, the distance and the masses are degenerate, and nothing in a purely magnified waveform separates them. A source that appears to sit at redshift one may in fact be a magnified source at redshift four, and there is no way to tell from the amplitude.

Diffraction is what breaks the degeneracy. A frequency-dependent distortion is set by the redshifted mass of the lens, which is an absolute scale rather than a ratio, so a diffracted signal says how much lens it passed and not merely that it was amplified. This is the same property that makes wave optics useful everywhere else on this site, applied to the question of where a source actually is.

The complication is that strong magnification and microlensing arrive together. A highly magnified image lies close to a caustic of the lensing galaxy, which is exactly where the galaxy's stars distort it most. So the events that reach furthest are also the ones whose interpretation depends most on modelling a population of microlenses — which is why the two topics are not separable in practice, and why the searches for them are the same searches.
