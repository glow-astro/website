A lens is a population, not an object

When a gravitational wave is strongly lensed by a galaxy, the galaxy is not the whole story. The image forms somewhere in that galaxy, and that place contains stars, stellar remnants and dark substructure — each one a lens in its own right, each adding its own diffraction to the signal. At the frequencies ground-based detectors observe, the objects that matter most weigh tens to hundreds of solar masses, which makes them rare enough that their number along any given line of sight is a matter of luck.

That stochasticity is the defining difficulty. There is no single microlensing waveform to search for, because the answer depends on a particular random arrangement of objects that nobody will ever measure. What can be predicted is the statistics: the distribution of distortions produced by a population of a given density and mass function.

Which is also why microlensing is not only an astrophysical nuisance. The statistics depend on what the population is made of, so the same framework that describes stars in a lens galaxy describes a population of dark-matter objects along the line of sight — compact objects producing faint repetitions of a signal, or low-mass halos whose collective distortion carries the imprint of the whole population. The lenses differ; the mathematics does not.

The environment cannot be separated out

A microlens does not act alone either. It sits inside the potential of the host galaxy, which magnifies the image it distorts. As the magnification produced by the galaxy rises, the same microlens imprints a far stronger signature. Highly magnified images — the ones most likely to be detected — are also the ones most strongly affected by microlensing.{% include cite.html key="mishra-microlensing" %}

So the macroscopic lens model and the microscopic population have to be fitted together. That is what makes the problem hard, and it is also what makes a detection informative: the distortion measures the environment as well as the objects.

Turning statistics into a search

The route from stochastic physics to a usable search is compression. Simulating many realizations of a stellar field produces many amplification curves; those curves are not arbitrary, and a small number of modes captures almost all of the variation between them. Fitting the coefficients of those modes turns an unpredictable lens into a handful of parameters, and the distribution of coefficients across realizations supplies a physically motivated prior for them.

This is the reduced-order model the project's searches are built on.{% include cite.html key="glow-code" %} It has already been applied to real data: GW231123 is the first compelling candidate for a magnified and diffracted black-hole merger.
