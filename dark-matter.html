The scales where the problem lives

On large scales, cold dark matter works: the distribution of galaxies and the cosmic microwave background are described by it to remarkable precision. The questions that distinguish between candidates — whether dark matter is a particle, a field, or a population of compact objects — live on much smaller scales, where the predictions of different models diverge and observations run out.

The obstacle is that these scales are, by construction, hard to see. Objects below the mass of a dwarf galaxy contain few stars or none at all, and conventional lensing cannot resolve them: an image cannot show structure smaller than the telescope can resolve.

Why diffraction reaches them

Diffraction is not limited by resolution. A wave is sensitive to any mass within the Fresnel scale of its path, and that scale is set by the wavelength, not by an instrument. An object that no telescope could image still leaves a frequency-dependent imprint on a wave that passes it closely enough.

The animation shows the mechanism. As the frequency rises, the region around the line of sight that contributes shrinks, and the number of halos inside it falls from thousands to a handful. Low frequencies aggregate the whole population into a collective distortion; high frequencies are shaped by the few objects that remain. One signal therefore carries information about a range of masses, and the way the distortion changes across the band is what distinguishes many light lenses from one heavy one.

What the project is looking for

Two regimes, on different instruments. With ground-based detectors the targets are individual dense objects — the kind predicted by self-interacting and ultra-light dark matter, or by primordial black holes that have accreted a halo — and the immediate task is to distinguish them from stars and stellar remnants, which produce similar signatures and are the dominant astrophysical systematic.

With LISA the target is the population itself. Thousands of low-mass halos along the line of sight to a massive black-hole binary leave a collective diffraction imprint whose fluctuations, rather than any individual feature, carry the signal. Making LISA's inference robust to that imprint, and then turning it into a measurement of cold-dark-matter substructure, is one of the project's principal goals.
