A lens as an experiment on gravity

Gravitational waves are a direct probe of gravity in a way light is not:{% include cite.html key="gw150914" %} they are gravity, propagating. Watching one travel across the Universe and past a lens tests the theory that describes both the propagation and the lensing, in one measurement.

The observation that a lensed gravitational wave arrives at all is already weakly informative, but it is not a demanding test — most alternatives to general relativity predict lensing too. The interesting question is whether the wave is distorted in ways that a lens made of ordinary matter cannot produce, obeying general relativity.

Signatures with no conventional counterpart

Theories that modify gravity generally introduce extra fields, and those fields respond to the mass of a lens. A wave passing through the region where a lens has excited such a field need not keep its polarization structure intact.

The animation shows one example: birefringence. If the two polarization states travel at slightly different speeds, one pulls ahead of the other. When the delay is long, the detector sees an echo; when it is short, the two copies overlap and the waveform is scrambled into something no unlensed source produces. Related effects give the propagation a frequency dependence that ordinary lensing does not.

These are qualitatively distinct signatures, not small corrections to a known shape, which is what makes them worth looking for even when the expected effect is small.

Connecting to dark energy

The same extra fields that would modify how a wave propagates past a lens are the ones invoked to explain the accelerating expansion of the Universe. A constraint on gravitational-wave propagation is therefore a constraint on dark-energy theories,{% include cite.html key="ezquiaga-dark-energy" %} and it applies where cosmological observations are weakest — on the scales of individual lenses rather than of the Universe as a whole.

The project will release these constraints as likelihoods usable with Einstein–Boltzmann solvers, so that they can be combined with cosmological data rather than quoted beside it.
