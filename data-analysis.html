What it takes to claim a lensed event

A lensed gravitational wave does not announce itself. It arrives as an ordinary-looking signal with a distortion in it, and the question is whether that distortion is better explained by a lens than by noise, by an unusual source, or by the limits of the waveform model being used. Answering it means fitting the lensed and the unlensed hypotheses to the same data and comparing them honestly.

That is harder than it sounds, because every ingredient is uncertain at once. The lens adds parameters to a source model that already has fifteen. The waveform model itself has systematic errors, and a lensing search must not mistake them for a lens. And the interesting candidates are usually faint, which is exactly where all of these effects are hardest to separate.

The animation is a picture of that fit. Each posterior sample is a lens configuration predicting its own signal, and the ridge in the left panel is the combination of lens mass and shear the data genuinely constrain — as opposed to the parameters that only look constrained because they were chosen conveniently.

Models that can be fitted

A stochastic lens cannot be fitted directly: nobody will ever measure the particular arrangement of stars that produced a given distortion. What can be fitted is a compressed description. Expanding the ratio of the lensed to the unlensed waveform in a basis of functions turns an unpredictable lens into a handful of coefficients, and the distribution of those coefficients across simulated realizations gives them a physically motivated prior.

The significance of a lensing claim then comes from the posteriors of those coefficients rather than from a template match. This has a useful property: the model is agnostic about what the lenses are, so the same fit serves stars and dark-matter objects, and the distinction between them is drawn afterwards, from the population the coefficients imply.

One event, or the whole catalog

Not every question is answered by a single loud event. Lensing also acts on a catalog as a whole: it changes the distribution of apparent masses, distances and signal strengths, and a population that has been lensed looks different in aggregate from one that has not. That makes the catalog itself a measurement, and one that survives even when no individual event is convincing on its own.

Population analyses also discipline the single-event claims. An astrophysical prior on how often lensing should occur, and on what the lenses are likely to be, is what turns a well-fitting lensed model into a credible one.

Why speed is a physics problem

Two things are growing at once. Lensing enlarges the parameter space of every analysis, and the detectors are moving towards thousands of events. Analyses that take weeks per event do not merely become inconvenient; they determine which questions can be asked at all. Subthreshold searches, where a diffracted signal may have been pushed below the detection threshold by the very distortion that makes it interesting, are only possible if each fit is cheap.

Machine-learning inference is the route to that. A network trained to map data directly to posteriors amortizes the cost: the expense moves into training, and each subsequent event is analyzed in seconds. The project builds on DINGO for this, and is validating it with the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA collaboration for use in low-latency alerts — where the results have to be right, and have to arrive while telescopes can still be pointed.
