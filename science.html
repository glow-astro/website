Lensing, again

Light traveling through the Universe is deflected and magnified by gravitational fields, occasionally producing multiple images of the same object. Gravitational lensing began as a prediction thought unlikely to be observed, and became one of astronomy's most productive tools: multiply imaged quasars, cosmography, maps of dark matter inside lens galaxies, exoplanets found by microlensing. It has now been seen across the electromagnetic spectrum, and each source and spectral range brings its own strengths.

Two new kinds of source are about to join that list, and they behave differently from anything lensed before.

Why coherence changes things

Gravitational waves and fast radio bursts are phase coherent. Where light from a distant galaxy arrives as an incoherent sum and the lens can only magnify or split it, a coherent signal carries its phase intact, and the lens distorts the wavefront itself. The result is a frequency-dependent — chromatic — distortion: diffraction and interference written directly into the observed signal. That distortion is a measurement of the lens, not merely evidence that one was there.

What gravitational waves add

They can be modeled from first principles, they probe gravity directly, and they are essentially unabsorbed on their way to us. A lensing signature in a waveform can therefore be predicted, fitted and believed.

What fast radio bursts add

Precise sky localization, and an interaction with ionized matter — plasma lensing — that turns them into a probe of the baryons that are otherwise hardest to see.

Because diffraction is sensitive to structure comparable in size to the wavelength, coherent sources reach lens masses and scales that image-based lensing cannot resolve. This is what makes them a probe of dark matter on sub-galactic scales, and of objects too small and too dark to find any other way.

Why now

The observational situation is changing quickly. The LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA network will soon deliver thousands of detections from compact binary mergers. Radio surveys — CHIME, DSA-2000, CHORD, BURSTT — are increasing the number of observed fast radio bursts by orders of magnitude. A first discovery of lensed gravitational waves and fast radio bursts is expected in the coming years.

What follows is a shift from discovery to population: detector upgrades, the next generation of ground-based observatories, and the SKA survey. LISA, adopted by ESA in 2024 and scheduled to launch in 2035, opens the low-frequency band and with it a different set of lensing signatures.

The methods have to exist before the events arrive. An advanced framework for realistic matter distributions — not the handful of idealized lenses with closed-form solutions — is what stands between a detection and an interpretation.

What GLOW will deliver

Three goals, and the methods that have to exist before they can be reached.

  • Discovery Find lensed gravitational waves, including sources at high redshift and in the vicinity of supermassive black holes
  • Fundamental physics Probe dark-matter objects, the nature of gravity, and theories of dark energy
  • Readiness Have the framework in place for upcoming fast-radio-burst surveys, next-generation detectors and multi-messenger follow-up

Methodological developments

  • Theory and numerical methods for microlensing and plasma diffraction (WP1)
  • Microlensed waveforms, population analysis and accelerated parameter estimation (WP2)
  • Effective description of dark-matter diffraction and gravitational-wave propagation (WP3)

The project builds on several years of work on wave-optics lensing and on the public GLoW code, which already computes diffraction by general matter distributions fast enough to sit inside a parameter-estimation loop.
