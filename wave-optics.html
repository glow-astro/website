When a lens stops being a magnifying glass

Light from a distant galaxy reaches us as an incoherent sum of countless independent emissions. A lens in the way can bend it, brighten it, and split it into separate images, but each image is still just a picture: the lens acts like a piece of glass, and the description that works is geometric optics — rays, paths, magnifications.

That description holds only while the wavelength is very small compared with everything the lens is made of. Gravitational waves from merging black holes have wavelengths of hundreds to thousands of kilometres, and the waves LISA will observe are larger still. Once the wavelength approaches the scale of the lens, the separate images stop being separate: their wavefronts overlap and interfere, and the result depends on frequency.{% include cite.html key="leung-wave-mechanics" %}

What the detector records is then the unlensed signal multiplied by an amplification factor — a complex, frequency-dependent function that encodes the lens. Its oscillations are the interference between paths; their spacing measures time delays, and their envelope measures how the mass is distributed. This is the quantity everything on this site is ultimately about.

Which lenses a wave can feel

Diffraction sets its own scale. A wave of a given frequency is sensitive to structure within a region around the line of sight — the Fresnel scale — that shrinks as the frequency rises. Mass outside it barely matters; mass inside it contributes coherently.

Two consequences follow, and both are why coherent sources are worth the trouble. A single signal probes a range of scales at once, because it spans a range of frequencies. And the scales it reaches are set by the wavelength rather than by any telescope's resolution, which is how a gravitational wave can be sensitive to objects far too small and too dark to image — the subject of dark matter.

Not only gravity: plasma and dispersion

A gravitational lens is not the only thing that can delay a wave by an amount that depends on its frequency. Ionized gas does it too: a radio pulse crossing a cloud of free electrons is refracted and dispersed, and where the gas is structured on small enough scales the same wave-optics treatment applies. This is plasma lensing, and for fast radio bursts it is not a correction but a probe in its own right{% include cite.html key="cordes-plasma" %} — a way of weighing ionized matter that is otherwise close to invisible.

The physics differs in one instructive way. Gravitational lensing is achromatic in the geometric limit, so all its frequency dependence comes from diffraction; plasma lensing is chromatic to begin with, because the refractive index of a plasma depends on frequency. The two therefore leave distinguishable signatures, and a lens that contains both can be taken apart.

Dispersion of a different kind appears again when gravity itself is modified, where a frequency-dependent propagation speed is one of the clearest signals that something beyond general relativity is at work.

Computing it for a real lens

For a handful of idealized lenses — a point mass, a perfectly symmetric halo — the amplification factor can be written down. Real lenses are neither. A galaxy contains stars, remnants and dark substructure; the integral that gives the amplification factor runs over a potential with singularities in it, and has to be evaluated fast enough to sit inside a parameter-estimation loop that will call it millions of times.{% include cite.html key="glow-code" %}

Making that tractable for general matter distributions is what the GLoW code does. Extending it to populations of lenses, and to the multi-plane geometry that plasma lensing needs, is the central methodological task of the project.
